# Script — 001

## Why Costco Checks Your Receipt Before You Leave

You already paid.

The cashier scanned the cart. Your card went through. You have a receipt in your hand.

Then you walk about thirty feet toward the exit... and Costco stops you again.

Someone looks at the receipt, looks at the cart, marks the paper, and sends you on your way.

It feels redundant.

And the obvious explanation is: theft.

But that is not actually how Costco explains the practice.

Costco's own customer-service page says the exit check is there to double-check that purchases were processed correctly by the cashier, to help keep inventory records accurate, and to make sure members were charged properly.

That sounds almost boring.

But once you understand how Costco makes money, the little check at the door starts to make a lot more sense.

Because Costco is not just a giant store.

It is a giant inventory machine attached to a membership business.

And both parts depend on accuracy.

Imagine a simple checkout mistake.

A case of drinks is under the cart and never gets scanned.

From the shopper's point of view, the bill is wrong.

From the store's point of view, something even stranger happens: the computer still thinks that case is sitting somewhere inside the warehouse, even though it just rolled out the door.

Now flip the mistake.

Suppose an item gets scanned twice.

The inventory count may be fine, but the member has been overcharged.

Neither situation is good.

One hurts inventory accuracy. The other hurts the customer.

The exit check creates a second control point before both sides leave the building.

And Costco is unusually explicit about this.

Its membership conditions say that receipts and merchandise are inspected when members leave so that members are correctly charged.

The policy separately says Costco can inspect bags and containers.

That separation matters.

It tells us we should not reduce the entire receipt ritual to one sentence: "Costco thinks you're stealing."

Security matters in retail. Costco even employs dedicated loss-prevention staff whose job descriptions explicitly talk about theft, shrink, investigations, and protecting company assets.

But when Costco explains the receipt check itself, the language is about processing and inventory accuracy.

Now zoom out.

Why would a company pay employees to stand at the exit checking purchases that were supposedly checked seconds earlier?

Because at Costco's scale, small operational errors stop being small.

In fiscal 2026, Costco reported more than two hundred ninety-seven billion dollars in net sales.

Its merchandise cost more than two hundred sixty-four billion dollars.

The difference — gross margin on the merchandise — was about thirty-three billion dollars.

That works out to roughly eleven cents of merchandise gross margin for every dollar of net sales before Costco pays the rest of the costs of running the business.

Costco itself says this is part of the design.

Its model is built around low prices, huge sales volumes, rapid inventory turnover, efficient distribution, limited handling, and no-frills warehouses.

That lets Costco operate at significantly lower gross margins than most retailers.

So picture what that means operationally.

You are not trying to make a huge margin on one fancy item.

You are trying to move an enormous amount of merchandise through the building efficiently and accurately.

Inventory comes in.

Inventory moves quickly.

Inventory leaves.

The system needs to know what actually happened.

If physical inventory and computer inventory drift apart, somebody eventually has to reconcile the difference.

And when you are moving hundreds of billions of dollars of merchandise, accuracy becomes infrastructure.

The door check is one tiny piece of that infrastructure.

But there is another reason Costco cares about the experience differently from a normal store.

You paid to be there.

At the end of fiscal 2026, Costco reported eighty-four point one million paid members.

It collected about five point nine billion dollars in membership-fee revenue during the year.

And its U.S. and Canadian renewal rate was ninety-two point three percent.

That renewal number is important.

Costco does not only need you to make today's purchase.

It wants you to decide, year after year, that the membership is still worth paying for.

That changes the relationship.

If a member gets double charged, discovering the mistake at home is frustrating.

If a transaction mismatch can be spotted before the member reaches the parking lot, the company at least has a chance to correct it while the customer is still there.

The same doorway can therefore serve two sides of the system.

On one side: did the transaction match what left the warehouse?

On the other: did the member pay for what they actually received?

That is much more interesting than a simple theft story.

And it also explains why Costco can tolerate something that would feel bizarre in many ordinary stores.

The receipt check is written into the membership conditions.

Members enter a private membership system with rules that include showing the membership card, following warehouse policies, and having receipts and merchandise inspected at the exit.

You may still find the ritual annoying.

You may think the employee barely looked at your cart.

And the quality of any individual check can obviously vary.

But the business logic does not require every check to be a minute-long audit.

It creates a visible second verification layer.

A second chance to catch mismatches.

A signal that checkout and inventory accuracy matter.

And yes, the presence of a checkpoint may also discourage some bad behavior.

But that is different from claiming Costco's official reason is simply "we don't trust you."

The company describes theft and loss prevention separately.

That distinction is exactly the kind of thing that gets lost when we explain businesses with one catchy sentence.

Costco is successful partly because a lot of individually boring systems work together.

Limited selection.

Large pack sizes.

High volume.

Fast inventory turnover.

Membership fees.

High renewal.

And at the very end of the trip, one employee with a marker and your receipt.

The check lasts a few seconds.

But it sits at the intersection of the two things Costco is trying to protect:

the accuracy of the merchandise system...

and the trust of the member who pays to come back.

So the next time someone marks your Costco receipt at the door, the better question is not:

"Do they think I stole something?"

It is:

"What is Costco trying to make sure happened correctly before this cart leaves the building?"

And according to Costco, the answer is surprisingly simple.

The transaction.

The inventory.

And your bill.
